Executive Verdict
A comprehensive synthesis of the primary literature across distributed systems, machine
learning, and decentralized network architectures has been conducted to evaluate the
proposed federated learning (FL) framework. The manuscript introduces a combined
architecture merging capacity-aware heterogeneous model assignment with a
blockchain-coordinated trust and aggregation layer. The rigorous evaluation of the five core
claims yields the following determinations regarding their novelty and integration into the
current state of the art:
● Claim 1 (Capacity-aware heterogeneous model assignment via throughput
benchmarking): Partially established. The overarching paradigm of distributing varying
model architectures to mitigate system heterogeneity is extensively documented in
foundational frameworks such as HeteroFL and FedRolex. However, the specific
deterministic operationalization—utilizing a benchmark throughput metric to map clients
to discrete capacity classes (e.g., strictly assigning MobileNet, EfficientNet, or
ResNet)—represents a highly pragmatic, albeit incremental, systems engineering choice
rather than a fundamental theoretical leap in federated optimization.1
● Claim 2 (Prediction-space aggregation via weighted softmax ensembling): Already
established. The transition from parameter-space to prediction-space aggregation is the
standard protocol for handling heterogeneous architectures. Frameworks such as FedMD
and the highly refined KTA v2 (Knowledge Trading Market) utilize identical mechanisms,
including logits sharing over public reference sets and similarity-accuracy weighted
ensembling, rendering this claim an established architectural enabler rather than a novel
invention.5
● Claim 3 (On-chain deterministic aggregation weight computation via capacity,
confidence, ECE, and history): Likely novel. While the individual constituent metrics
(Expected Calibration Error, confidence bounds, participation history) are utilized in
off-chain aggregations or isolated vehicular trust management systems, synthesizing
them into a deterministic, on-chain smart contract computation for FL aggregation
weights constitutes a highly original and technically demanding contribution. This
mechanism effectively pioneers a cryptoeconomically secure meritocracy for model
updates.7
● Claim 4 (Blockchain used for coordination with only model hash + scalar metrics
on-chain): Already established. Anchoring only cryptographic commitments and scalar
metadata on-chain while offloading dense parameter tensors to decentralized storage is

the de facto standard for modern blockchain-enabled FL and Zero-Knowledge Machine
Learning (ZKML) systems. It is an engineering necessity to circumvent catastrophic gas

● Claim 5 (Signed benchmark-hash participant registration as weak

Deconstruction of Target Claims and Literature
Alignment
The intersection of federated learning and decentralized ledger technology aims to resolve
inherent vulnerabilities in traditional FL, namely centralized single points of failure, lack of
immutable auditability, and susceptibility to adversarial model poisoning. Concurrently, the
proliferation of Internet of Things (IoT) devices and edge computing introduces severe system
and statistical heterogeneity, complicating collaborative learning. The proposed system
attempts to unify these disparate vectors into a cohesive pipeline. The following subsections
critically analyze each claim against the state of the art, dissecting the mathematical,
architectural, and systemic precedents.
Claim 1: Capacity-Aware Heterogeneous Model Assignment
Target Claim: The system maps clients to fixed architectures (MobileNet, EfficientNet, ResNet)
by benchmarking throughput to determine a capacity class.
Classification: Partially established.
In classical federated learning formulations, such as the seminal Federated Averaging (FedAvg)
and its proximal variant FedProx, the underlying mathematical assumption is strict model
homogeneity. The central server and all participating edge clients must possess identical neural
network architectures to facilitate parameter exchange.15 This assumption introduces a severe
operational bottleneck in real-world deployments known as the "straggler effect." Because
client devices range from high-performance edge servers to low-power microcontrollers, the
global training round is gated by the compute and network latency of the weakest participating
node. Furthermore, attempting to force a highly parameterized model onto a constrained
device leads to out-of-memory errors, effectively excluding a massive swath of potential data
contributors from the federated network.18
To mitigate these limitations, recent literature has aggressively pursued model-heterogeneous
federated learning. The proposed methodology assigns distinct, well-established Convolutional
Neural Network (CNN) architectures based on an initial throughput benchmark. This mirrors a

-- 2 of 23 --

highly active area of research, though it diverges slightly in its specific implementation.
Foundational frameworks such as HeteroFL directly challenge the homogeneity assumption by
adaptively distributing subnetworks of varying computation complexities based on client
hardware capabilities.4 Similarly, the FedRolex framework introduces a rolling sub-model
extraction technique, permitting low-end devices to iteratively train sequential portions of a
much larger global model, thereby democratizing participation without demanding hardware
parity.3
However, both HeteroFL and FedRolex typically rely on extracting sub-networks from a single,
unified super-network architecture. The proposed pipeline—which dictates the assignment of
entirely distinct, disjoint architectures such as MobileNet, EfficientNet, and ResNet—aligns
more closely with the domain of federated mutual learning or Knowledge Distillation
(KD)-based federated learning. In these paradigms, clients are entirely agnostic to the
architectures of their peers. Systems like HAPFNAS (Heterogeneity-Aware Personalized
Federated Neural Architecture Search) utilize resource-aware clustering algorithms to
automatically discover and deploy varying, optimal architectures tailored to the specific
hardware constraints of disparate clients.23
The operationalization of this assignment via empirical throughput benchmarking is a sound
engineering practice heavily documented in hardware evaluation literature. Extensive studies
benchmarking inference frameworks (e.g., PyTorch, ONNX Runtime, TensorRT) on platforms
like the NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin explicitly utilize throughput metrics (samples processed per
second) to delineate the capacity boundaries of varying architectures.

Claim 3: On-Chain Deterministic Aggregation Weight Computation
Target Claim: Computing aggregation weights deterministically on-chain utilizing a
multi-variate function of capacity, confidence, Expected Calibration Error (ECE), and
participation history.
Classification: Likely novel.
This claim represents the absolute core contribution and the crown jewel of the proposed
architecture. In traditional federated learning paradigms, aggregation weights are computed
off-chain by a central, trusted server. Typically, these weights are derived using highly simplistic
metrics, most commonly proportional to the raw number of local data samples contributed by
each client (as seen in the FedAvg algorithm).15 In adversarial, decentralized, or zero-trust
environments, this centralized aggregation methodology introduces a severe vulnerability. It
relies entirely on the honesty of the central aggregator and assumes that high data volume
correlates directly with high model quality, ignoring the reality of data poisoning, label flipping,
and catastrophic overfitting.
Transitioning the aggregation logic to an on-chain smart contract fundamentally alters the trust
model, enforcing strict transparency, immutability, and auditability over how the global model is
synthesized. However, executing complex mathematical algorithms within the confines of a
smart contract (such as the Ethereum Virtual Machine) is fraught with extreme deterministic
constraints. Smart contracts lack access to native floating-point arithmetic and are bound by
highly restrictive gas limits, rendering complex matrix operations or exponential math
prohibitively expensive and computationally challenging.
The proposed system addresses these constraints by computing the aggregation weight
based on a multivariate fusion of four distinct, carefully selected scalar metrics:
1. Capacity: Derived deterministically from the initial network vetting and registration
phase.
2. Confidence: The raw softmax probability outputs representing the model's certainty in
its predictions.
3. Expected Calibration Error (ECE): A critical statistical metric that quantifies the
misalignment between a model's stated confidence and its actual empirical accuracy. It
serves to punish models that are highly confident but consistently wrong.
4. Participation History: A longitudinal, historical metric of node reliability and uptime,
effectively serving as a decentralized reputation score.
The synthesis of these specific metrics within an on-chain environment is highly sophisticated.

Executing the ECE calculation on-chain requires advanced fixed-point approximations and

challenge is akin to recent advancements in Zero-Knowledge Machine Learning (ZKML), where
models must be quantized and mathematically approximated to generate valid cryptographic
proofs.31 By binding ECE—which severely penalizes overconfident, poorly calibrated, or
potentially poisoned models—with participation history—which penalizes erratic,
unpredictable, or intermittent clients—the proposed smart contract creates a highly robust,
Byzantine-fault-tolerant aggregation logic.
This mechanism effectively instantiates an automated, cryptoeconomically secure
"meritocracy" for model updates that is objectively superior to naive sample-size weighting.8
The deterministic combination of advanced calibration metrics (ECE) and historical fidelity,
successfully mapped into a gas-efficient smart contract, stands as a significant, highly
publishable systems contribution that bridges the gap between deep learning reliability theory
and decentralized consensus.
Claim 4: Blockchain Coordination via Model Hash + Scalar Metrics
Target Claim: Utilizing the blockchain solely for coordination and auditability, storing only
model hashes and scalar metrics on-chain rather than full parameters.
Classification: Already established.
The storage of full neural network parameters on a distributed ledger is technically unfeasible
and practically impossible under current blockchain architectures. Modern deep learning
architectures, even those heavily compressed and tailored for resource-constrained edge
devices like ResNet-18, EfficientNet, or MobileNetV3, contain millions of parameters resulting in
tens of megabytes of dense floating-point tensors.2 Writing this volume of data to a blockchain
block—which must be propagated, verified, and perpetually stored across thousands of
globally distributed consensus nodes—would incur catastrophic gas costs, violate block size
limitations, and bloat the chain state irreparably.12
Therefore, the practice of decoupling the heavy data layer from the lightweight consensus
layer is ubiquitous in the current literature. Frameworks such as Secure Interpretable Federated
Learning (SIFL) explicitly dictate this design pattern: "On-chain content Hashes/metadata only...
SIFL keeps storage/latency minimal, while some push heavy payloads on-chain".10 Similarly, the

-- 7 of 23 --

LiteChain framework optimizes on-chain storage overheads specifically for massive edge
networks, ensuring that the ledger remains agile and scalable.32
In the broader context of verifiable computation, decentralized physical infrastructure
networks (DePIN), and ZKML, this paradigm is strictly defined as a foundational engineering
requirement. As detailed in recent overviews of on-chain operations: "On-Chain Data
Minimization: The on-chain workflow stores only compact commitments (hashes) and agreed
metric outputs, not raw datasets. Evaluation data is provided to vendors out-of-band... This is
an engineering property that reduces on-chain storage costs and avoids unnecessary data
publication".11 The actual model weights, sub-networks, or logits are uniformly stored in
off-chain distributed file systems like IPFS (InterPlanetary File System) or Swarm. Only the
Merkle root or the cryptographic commitment is anchored to the smart contract to guarantee
integrity, provenance, and non-repudiation. Thus, Claim 4 represents adherence to a standard
architectural best practice in decentralized FL, not a novel design pattern or theoretical
discovery.
Claim 5: Signed Benchmark-Hash Participant Registration
Target Claim: Using a signed benchmark-hash during participant registration as a weak
proof-of-capacity vetting mechanism.
Classification: Partially established / Likely novel specific variant.
In permissionless, decentralized federated learning environments, the system is highly
vulnerable to Sybil attacks. In a Sybil attack, a single malicious adversary spins up thousands of
inexpensive virtual nodes or spoofed identities to artificially inflate their influence over the
network, effectively hijacking the aggregation process or poisoning the global model by
overwhelming honest updates.33 Traditional blockchains counter Sybil attacks via
resource-intensive consensus mechanisms such as Proof of Work (PoW) or Proof of Stake
(PoS).34 However, both paradigms present severe drawbacks in the context of federated
learning. PoW wastes immense computational energy executing arbitrary cryptographic
puzzles—energy that should be strictly reserved for training the neural networks. PoS
centralizes influence among wealthy nodes who hold the most tokens, which is antithetical to
the data-driven, egalitarian nature of federated learning where diverse edge data is the true
asset.
The proposed system mandates that prospective nodes submit a "signed benchmark-hash"
upon initial registration. This mechanism acts as a cryptographic attestation of the node's
actual hardware capabilities (throughput). It serves as a domain-specific, lightweight Proof of
Capacity (PoC). By forcing the client to solve a standardized, pre-defined machine learning
benchmarking task and cryptographically signing the resultant hash, the system achieves two
critical objectives simultaneously:
1. It accurately and objectively categorizes the node for the execution of Claim 1
(capacity-aware model assignment), ensuring that ResNet models are not erroneously
routed to MobileNet-tier hardware.

2. It imposes a minor, domain-relevant computational hurdle that severely deters rapid,
large-scale Sybil generation without wasting compute on useless hash-collisions.
While the broader concept of utilizing attestations, stakes, or verifiable credentials for
admission control is heavily documented (particularly within DAO governance models, liquid
democracy, and ZKML contributor admission systems that compute multi-dimensional
stakeholder legitimacy) 8, formulating the registration protocol specifically around a signed
benchmark-hash tailored to neural network throughput is an elegant, pragmatic innovation. It
creates a "weak" but highly effective barrier to entry that is directly coupled to the functional,
physical requirements of the machine learning network. This represents a highly specific,
moderately novel variant of decentralized admission control that bridges identity protocols
with hardware profiling.

Based on the novelty matrix and extensive literature synthesis, the three closest competing
works to the proposed system are KTA v2, SIFL, and HeteroFL/FedRolex. Understanding the
exact architectural overlaps and the mathematical gaps is critical for pinpointing the
manuscript's unique contributions.
1. KTA v2 (Knowledge Trading Market version 2)

Overlap: KTA v2 completely subsumes Claim 2. It is a highly optimized, state-of-the-art
prediction-space knowledge market designed explicitly for communication-efficient
heterogeneous FL. It dictates that clients train locally on private data and share only their logits
on a public reference set. Furthermore, it explicitly employs personalized weighted ensembling
based on prediction-space similarity metrics and reference-set accuracy, proving that soft

immutable auditability, and does not compute weights via deterministic on-chain logic.

and it lacks a capacity-aware initial assignment mechanism, assuming clients already possess
their arbitrarily heterogeneous models.
2. SIFL (Secure Interpretable Federated Learning)
Overlap: SIFL tightly overlaps with Claim 4 and aspects of Claims 3 and 5. It is a robust
framework that integrates advanced FL optimization with blockchain verification. Crucially, SIFL
already practices strict on-chain data minimization by storing only hashes and metadata
on-chain to keep storage and latency minimal, effectively anticipating Claim 4.10 SIFL also
heavily evaluates Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and model confidence bounds, integrating
these complex metrics into a unified post-hoc interpretability and security pipeline.10 Gap:
While SIFL monitors ECE and leverages blockchain infrastructure, it remains bound to
conventional parameter-averaging optimizers (such as FedAvg, FedProx, and FedNova) rather
than prediction-space aggregation.10 Consequently, it cannot easily accommodate extreme
architecture heterogeneity. Furthermore, its blockchain component is utilized primarily for
tamper-evident logging and legacy Proof of Work (PoW) consensus, rather than for executing
the aggregation logic itself via a deterministic smart contract based on a multivariate
capacity/confidence equation.
3. HeteroFL / FedRolex
Overlap: These foundational frameworks overlap significantly with Claim 1. They explicitly
tackle the pervasive issue of system heterogeneity by adaptively distributing varying model
complexities to clients based on their inherent hardware capabilities, ensuring that stragglers

mechanism: extracting smaller sub-networks from a single global super-network architecture.

Overclaim Corrections and Remediation
The manuscript currently risks severe critique and potential rejection in high-tier academic
venues if it presents all five claims as fundamentally novel inventions. Precision in academic
claims is paramount. The authors must carefully recalibrate their assertions to reflect the
existing state of the art accurately, ensuring they do not claim ownership of paradigms
established by prior researchers.
Correction 1: Prediction-Space Aggregation (Claim 2)
● Current Posture: The manuscript implies that utilizing weighted softmax ensembling in
the prediction space is a novel substitute for parameter averaging in heterogeneous FL
environments.
● Critique: This constitutes a severe overclaim. Federated Knowledge Distillation via logits
sharing and weighted ensembling has been firmly established since the introduction of
FedMD 6 and has been highly perfected in modern systems such as KTA v2.5
● Remediation: The authors must demote this claim from an "invention" to an "architectural
enabler." They should explicitly cite KTA v2 and FedMD in their methodology,
acknowledging that prediction-space aggregation is the chosen, established
communication protocol that allows their truly novel on-chain smart contract to function
without requiring massive parameter transfers.
Correction 2: On-Chain Data Minimization (Claim 4)
● Current Posture: Suggests that using the blockchain strictly for coordination—storing
only model hashes and scalar metrics while offloading heavy tensors—is a novel
architectural contribution.
● Critique: Storing only hashes and cryptographic commitments on-chain while offloading
heavy data is the standard operating procedure for every competent blockchain-FL and
ZKML system deployed in the last three years (e.g., SIFL, LiteChain, On-Chain ZKML).10
Claiming this as novel displays a concerning lack of awareness regarding standard Web3
engineering practices and EVM constraints.
● Remediation: Reframe this element as a "design constraint" rather than a feature. State
that the system "strictly adheres to standard on-chain data minimization principles to
maintain vital gas efficiency and block propagation speeds."

Recommended Claim Wording for Abstract and
Introduction
To align the manuscript with the rigorous standards of top-tier academic venues and properly
frame the contributions, the abstract and introduction should be restructured to highlight the
exact intersectional novelty of the work. The following phrasing is highly recommended:
"The proliferation of system and statistical heterogeneity in edge environments
fundamentally limits classical parameter-averaging Federated Learning (FL). While
recent advances in Federated Knowledge Distillation have successfully utilized
prediction-space aggregation to accommodate heterogeneous architectures,
orchestrating these updates in adversarial, zero-trust environments remains a
critical vulnerability.
To address this, we introduce a fully decentralized, blockchain-coordinated FL
architecture that synthesizes capacity-aware model assignment with
cryptoeconomic security. We do not claim novelty in prediction-space ensembling
or on-chain data minimization; rather, we build upon these established paradigms
to introduce two specific systemic innovations. First, we propose a lightweight
Sybil defense mechanism utilizing a signed benchmark-hash participant
registration, which serves as an automated Proof-of-Capacity to deterministically
assign clients to discrete architecture classes (e.g., MobileNet, ResNet). Second,
and most crucially, we introduce a deterministic on-chain smart contract for
aggregation weight computation. By translating local model outputs (logits) into
scalar metrics, our smart contract securely calculates aggregation weights via a
multivariate function of the client's vetted capacity, prediction confidence,
Expected Calibration Error (ECE), and historical participation fidelity. This approach
effectively pioneers a mathematically rigorous, Byzantine-fault-tolerant
meritocracy for heterogeneous FL aggregation, executable entirely within the
stringent deterministic limits of decentralized ledgers."
Publishability Risk and Final Assessment
Overall Novelty Assessment: Moderately Novel to Strongly Novel (in systems synthesis).
Justification:
If evaluated purely through the lens of algorithmic machine learning theory, the paper is
incremental; it reuses standard CNN architectures, standard KD ensembling equations, and

standard calibration metrics. However, if evaluated as a Distributed Systems / Decentralized
Network Architecture paper, it is strongly novel. The engineering acumen required to map
complex statistical reliability metrics (such as ECE and confidence bounds) into a deterministic,
gas-efficient on-chain smart contract, while simultaneously orchestrating heterogeneous
models via prediction-space distillation and defending against Sybil attacks with a novel
Proof-of-Capacity hash, represents a highly sophisticated and non-trivial synthesis. The whole
is significantly greater than the sum of its parts.
Publishability Risk List:
1. High Risk - Literature Ignorance: If the authors fail to extensively cite KTA v2, FedMD,
HeteroFL, and SIFL, reviewers at top-tier venues (e.g., NeurIPS, ICLR, IEEE TDSC, IEEE
TMC) will immediately flag the paper for ignoring prior art regarding prediction-space
aggregation and on-chain hashing. Proper attribution is non-negotiable.
2. High Risk - Smart Contract Gas Analysis: Because Claim 3 relies heavily on on-chain
computation, the authors must provide a rigorous, empirical gas-cost analysis (measured
in Gwei/USD equivalents) for the deterministic calculation of the ECE and participation
history logic. If this computation exceeds Ethereum block gas limits or is prohibitively
expensive to execute per round, the entire architecture becomes theoretical and
practically useless for real-world deployment.11 Reviewers will aggressively attack the
feasibility of on-chain floating-point/fixed-point approximations if data is absent.
3. Medium Risk - Reference Dataset Privacy and Availability: Prediction-space
aggregation (Claim 2) relies fundamentally on clients executing inferences and sharing
logits over a public reference set ( 	). The authors must briefly but decisively address
the data-availability and privacy implications of this reference set. Who curates it? How is
it distributed? Does it leak domain-specific information? This is a known vulnerability
vector in KD-based FL and will be questioned.5
4. Low Risk - Proof of Capacity Evasion: Reviewers may question whether the "signed
benchmark-hash" (Claim 5) can be spoofed by a sophisticated adversary utilizing
high-end cloud compute to artificially pass the benchmark and secure a high capacity
class, but subsequently deploying a low-tier device for actual training to save resources.
The authors should preemptively acknowledge this limitation, noting that the
"participation history" and "ECE" weight parameters (Claim 3) will naturally detect the
resultant drop in quality and gracefully decay the influence of such deceptive nodes over
time.
