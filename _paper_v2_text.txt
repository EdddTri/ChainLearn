Abstract—Federated learning is increasingly adopted in med-
ical imaging environments where privacy constraints prohibit
centralized data collection, yet most practical federated al-
gorithms assume homogeneous participant hardware, identical
model architectures, and centrally trusted aggregation. These
assumptions become restrictive when collaborating institutions
possess unequal computational resources and cannot sustain
identical neural network backbones. This work presents a
capacity-aware federated learning coordination framework in
which participating hospitals are assigned heterogeneous model
architectures according to measured local compute through-
put and coordinated through a blockchain-defined policy layer.
Rather than enforcing parameter-space compatibility, the system
performs weighted ensemble inference across heterogeneous local
models, enabling lightweight and high-capacity institutions to
jointly participate without architectural homogenization.
The proposed design separates on-chain coordination from
off-chain model execution. A Solidity smart contract stores
hospital registration metadata, signed benchmark hashes, per-
round scalar reliability submissions, and deterministic aggrega-
tion weights derived from declared capacity class, confidence,
calibration error, and participation history. Local hospitals
train architecture-specific convolutional networks selected from
MobileNetV3-Small, EfficientNet-B0, and ResNet-50, submit only
model hashes and scalar metrics, and retain all model parameters
off chain. Final inference is executed off chain through weighted
probability aggregation.
The implementation targets privacy-preserving medical image
classification using PneumoniaMNIST and DermaMNIST and
compares the proposed method against centralized training, local-
only baselines, FedAvg, FedProx, FedMD, equal-weight ensem-
bling, a dropout-aware variant, and multiple ablations under
controlled non-IID partitions. Across five random seeds and three
non-IID severity levels on both datasets, the proposed method
consistently achieves lower calibration error than equal-weight
ensembling and competitive accuracy against all federated base-
lines, while reducing per-round coordination payload from 204
MB (FedAvg) to 224 bytes—a reduction of over 900,000×. Cur-
rent prototype limitations include self-reported reliability metrics
and heuristic benchmark attestation. The primary contribution
is therefore an auditable heterogeneous federated coordination
architecture rather than a cryptographically complete proof-of-
capacity system or a new federated optimization algorithm.

I. INTRODUCTION
Federated learning has become a central paradigm for col-
laborative machine learning in domains where raw data cannot
be centrally aggregated. In healthcare systems, this constraint
is especially strong because clinical imaging repositories are
protected by institutional governance, privacy regulation, and
operational policy. Hospitals often possess diagnostically valu-
able local datasets but cannot directly exchange patient images,
motivating decentralized optimization in which local models
are trained independently and periodically coordinated through
aggregation.
The majority of practical federated learning systems, how-
ever, remain built around an implicit homogeneity assumption:
all participants train identical architectures under approxi-
mately comparable computational conditions. In canonical
parameter-averaging frameworks such as FedAvg, local model
tensors must share identical dimensional structure for aggre-
gation to remain valid. This assumption is difficult to satisfy
in realistic hospital networks. A tertiary medical center may
possess server-grade GPU infrastructure capable of sustaining
deep residual architectures, while smaller institutions may
operate under CPU-only constraints or lightweight edge en-
vironments. Under such conditions, architecture uniformity
either excludes weaker participants or underutilizes stronger
ones.
This mismatch creates a structural limitation in conventional
federated optimization. If all participants must train the same
network, the chosen architecture is determined by the weakest
participant. If stronger institutions independently train larger
models, standard parameter averaging becomes mathemati-
cally invalid because tensor dimensions no longer align. The
practical consequence is that heterogeneous compute environ-
ments force a compromise between participation breadth and
model expressiveness.
This work addresses that limitation through a capacity-
aware heterogeneous federated workflow in which model ar-
chitecture selection is itself treated as a coordination variable.
Instead of assuming identical local networks, participating
hospitals are first benchmarked through a local throughput
procedure and assigned one of three compute classes: weak,
medium, or strong. Each class maps deterministically to
a distinct convolutional backbone. Weak participants train
MobileNetV3-Small, medium participants train EfficientNet-
B0, and strong participants train ResNet-50. Because these ar-
chitectures occupy incompatible parameter spaces, aggregation
occurs not through weight averaging but through probability-

space ensembling.
The second challenge concerns coordination trust. Tradi-
tional federated learning relies on a centralized aggregator
that determines round participation, receives local updates,
and applies aggregation policy. Such a design centralizes
authority over participation semantics and leaves weighting
logic externally opaque. In this repository, coordination policy
is encoded in a blockchain smart contract implemented in
Solidity. The contract stores hospital registration state, signed
benchmark hashes, declared capacity classes, scalar reliability
metrics, and deterministic aggregation weights. Model tensors
never appear on chain; only hashes and scalar metadata are
recorded.
This distinction is critical. The blockchain layer does not ex-
ecute learning and does not perform inference. Instead, it acts
as an auditable coordination substrate in which policy remains
deterministic and externally inspectable. Hospitals submit
model hashes, confidence estimates, expected calibration error,
and architecture identifiers. The contract then applies a fixed-
point arithmetic weighting rule combining capacity multiplier,
calibration-adjusted confidence, and participation history. Final
ensemble inference remains off chain, where local model
outputs are combined through weighted probability averaging.
The present system therefore contributes at the level of
distributed coordination rather than optimization theory. It
does not introduce a new gradient method, nor does it
claim cryptographically secure proof-of-capacity attestation.
The benchmark procedure currently binds a signed benchmark
hash to hospital identity but does not allow the contract
to verify throughput correctness. Similarly, scalar reliability
metrics remain self-reported. Experimental evaluation further
uses a Python mirror of the contract-defined weighting rule
rather than live Solidity execution for the main research
benchmarks. These distinctions are essential for claim-safe
scientific interpretation.
The strongest contribution of the work is thus an auditable
heterogeneous federated coordination architecture with mini-
mal on-chain metadata and deterministic policy semantics. The
strongest limitation is that trust assumptions remain partially
externalized.
The contributions of this paper are threefold:
1) We introduce a capacity-aware heterogeneous federated
coordination workflow in which participants are as-
signed distinct model architectures according to mea-
sured local compute throughput, allowing institutions
with unequal hardware resources to jointly participate
without parameter-space compatibility requirements.
2) We design a blockchain coordination layer that stores
identity-bound submissions, enforces architecture-class
consistency, and deterministically computes aggrega-
tion weights from declared capacity, self-reported confi-
dence, calibration error, and participation history while
retaining all model parameters off chain.
3) We implement a communication-minimal off-chain
weighted ensemble strategy that aggregates heteroge-
neous model outputs in probability space and evaluate
it against multiple federated baselines under non-IID
medical image classification settings.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Classical Federated Learning
Federated learning emerged as a communication-efficient
alternative to centralized distributed optimization by enabling
local gradient computation while preserving data locality.
The most widely adopted baseline remains FedAvg, in which
local participants perform several gradient steps and transmit
parameter tensors to a central aggregator for weighted aver-
aging. Under homogeneous architectures and comparable data
distributions, this approach remains highly effective.
However, FedAvg depends fundamentally on parameter
compatibility:
θglobal =

where all θi must share identical tensor structure.
This requirement becomes restrictive when clients differ in
hardware capacity or model family selection. Even moderate
architecture variation invalidates direct tensor aggregation.
B. Federated Learning Under Heterogeneity
Several extensions attempt to relax FedAvg assumptions
under statistical or systems heterogeneity. FedProx introduces
proximal regularization to stabilize local optimization under
unequal participant behavior. This improves convergence when
local objectives differ but still assumes compatible architec-
tures.
FedMD moves coordination into output space by exchang-
ing consensus probabilities rather than model parameters. This
permits heterogeneous models to share predictive information
through temperature-scaled probability alignment rather than
tensor averaging. The present work is conceptually closer to
output-space coordination than parameter-space optimization,
although it differs by introducing deterministic blockchain-
defined weighting rather than distillation-driven consensus
refinement.
Unlike many heterogeneous FL methods that still assume
centralized orchestration, the present system explicitly exter-
nalizes policy into a smart contract.
C. Blockchain-Based Federated Coordination
Blockchain has been repeatedly proposed as a trust-
reduction mechanism for federated learning, particularly for
participant registration and immutable logging. Many prior
systems, however, overextend blockchain responsibilities by
implying on-chain model coordination at scales incompatible
with realistic neural parameter sizes.
A major practical weakness in much blockchain-FL litera-
ture is excessive on-chain payload size. Large convolutional
backbones cannot be economically stored on chain, and direct
parameter exchange through smart contracts is computation-
ally infeasible.

D. Federated Learning for Medical Imaging
Medical imaging remains one of the most compelling do-
mains for federated learning because privacy barriers strongly
prohibit centralized pooling of patient scans. Yet hospital
networks also exhibit strong infrastructure asymmetry. Large
tertiary hospitals frequently possess significantly stronger
compute than smaller institutions.
This asymmetry creates exactly the heterogeneous deploy-
ment environment that classical homogeneous FL does not
naturally address. The present work therefore uses medical
image classification not merely as a benchmark domain but as
an operationally realistic heterogeneous coordination scenario.

IV. SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE
The proposed framework is organized as a three-layer exe-
cution architecture aligned with trust boundaries and runtime
responsibilities. Rather than treating blockchain as a computa-
tional substrate for machine learning, the design deliberately
isolates heavy numerical learning from policy coordination.
This separation is necessary because neural model execution
remains computationally unsuitable for smart contract environ-
ments, while coordination semantics benefit from deterministic
externalization.
The complete architecture consists of:
1) an on-chain coordination and policy layer,
2) an off-chain hospital execution layer,
3) an experimental orchestration layer used for research
evaluation.
These layers correspond directly to the repository structure:
• smart_contracts/
• hospital_node/
• simulation/
This repository-level separation is not merely organiza-
tional; it reflects distinct operational trust domains.

A. On-Chain Coordination Layer
The blockchain layer is implemented through the Solidity
contract FLCoordinator. Its purpose is not to store models
or execute inference, but to encode deterministic participation
policy and persistent round metadata.
The contract maintains four primary categories of state:
• hospital registration metadata,
• per-round submissions,
• round submitter lists,
• ensemble records.
A hospital entry stores:
Hi = (addri, namei, ci, registeredi, hi, Ci, Ei, ri) (17)
where:
• addri denotes Ethereum identity,
• ci denotes declared capacity class,
• hi denotes benchmark hash,
• Ci denotes latest confidence,
• Ei denotes latest calibration error,
• ri denotes rounds participated.
The contract therefore acts as a deterministic state machine
rather than a federated optimizer.
B. Off-Chain Hospital Layer
Each hospital executes all computationally expensive oper-
ations locally:
• benchmark generation,
• model training,
• reliability computation,
• model hashing,
• blockchain transaction signing.
This logic resides primarily in:
• capacity_manager.py
• blockchain_client.py
• contract_integration.py
The hospital layer therefore contains the full learning path,
while the blockchain layer observes only scalar summaries.
C. Experiment Layer
The research evaluation layer introduces controlled bench-
marking independent of live contract execution.
This layer includes:
• baseline training,
• non-IID partition generation,
• ablation analysis,
• communication estimates,
• gas estimates,
• adversarial simulation.
This layer is implemented across three scripts with distinct
scopes:
• experiment.py — comprehensive evaluation: seven
baselines, five ablations, three non-IID levels, five seeds,
adversarial scenarios, communication and gas cost anal-
ysis. Mirrors contract weight logic in Python rather than
invoking Solidity in every training loop.
• run_simulation.py — end-to-end blockchain pro-
tocol demonstration: starts a local Hardhat node, deploys
FLCoordinator, registers hospitals with ECDSA-
signed PoC hashes, runs multi-round training, submits
updates on-chain, reads contract-computed weights, and
records ensemble prediction hashes on-chain. Final en-
semble is evaluated on a random tensor input (protocol
correctness, not predictive quality).
• simulate_federation.py — lightweight FedAvg
protocol demonstration: simulates homogeneous feder-
ated learning using a simple FederatedCNN on syn-
thetic data partitions. Serves as a standalone protocol
sanity check independent of the heterogeneous system.
This distinction is critical: contract policy and research
evaluation share identical arithmetic semantics but differ in
runtime execution environment.

IX. EXPERIMENTAL METHODOLOGY
A. Datasets
Two medical imaging datasets from MedMNIST are used
for evaluation.
PneumoniaMNIST contains binary chest X-ray classifica-
tion examples (normal/pneumonia). The training set contains
4,708 images; the official test set of 624 images is divided
50/50 into a validation split (312 images, used only for
computing reliability metrics and aggregation weights) and a
held-out test split (312 images, used only for final evaluation).
DermaMNIST contains dermatoscopy images across 7 skin
lesion classes, with the same 50/50 validation/test split applied
to its official test set.
Both datasets begin as 28 × 28 grayscale images and are
transformed to 224 × 224 × 3 for ImageNet-compatible archi-
tectures. The preprocessing pipeline applies tensor conversion,
channel expansion, resizing, and normalization.
The use of a dedicated validation split for reliability met-
ric computation ensures that aggregation weights are never
informed by the test data used for final reporting.
B. Data Partitioning
Two forms of data heterogeneity are implemented.
1) Fixed Label Skew: The hospital demonstration pipeline
uses manually skewed label partitions.
2) Dirichlet Non-IID Splits: The experiment engine uses
Dirichlet sampling:
α ∈ {1.0, 0.5, 0.1} (55)
where lower α implies stronger heterogeneity.
This allows controlled study of non-IID degradation.
C. Experimental Runtime Separation
A critical distinction must be preserved.
The main experiments do not execute Solidity directly
during every training loop.
Instead:
Wi = g(ci, Ci, Ei, ri) (56)
is mirrored in Python using the same fixed-point arithmetic
as the contract.
Thus the experiments evaluate:
contract-defined policy
rather than:
contract-executed runtime performance
This distinction is necessary for claim-safe interpretation.
D. Reliability Metric Extraction
Reliability metrics are computed on the dedicated validation
split:
(Ci, Ei) ← val loader (57)
The held-out test split is reserved exclusively for final
accuracy, F1, and ECE reporting. This separation ensures that
aggregation weights are not informed by the evaluation data,
enabling claim-safe generalization benchmarking.

XIV. EXPERIMENTAL INTERPRETATION BOUNDARY
The strongest experimentally defensible conclusion is:
heterogeneous capacity-aware weighted ensembling
consistently achieves lower calibration error than
equal-weight ensembling across both datasets and
all three non-IID regimes, and produces competitive
accuracy against all federated baselines.
The weaker claim that should be avoided is:
the proposed weighting formula universally domi-
nates established federated baselines under all eval-
uation conditions.
Under severe non-IID conditions on PneumoniaMNIST,
FedAvg achieves competitive accuracy (0.778 vs. 0.654 for
Ours). On DermaMNIST, the accuracy margins are small
across all methods due to the harder 7-class task. The No
PoC ablation can outperform the full system under moderate
non-IID when architectural diversity is simulated on a single
host. These observations do not invalidate the coordination
contribution but do define the boundary of predictive claims.
XV. COMMUNICATION AND COORDINATION COST
ANALYSIS
A. Why Communication Cost Matters in Heterogeneous FL
One of the strongest practical motivations for the proposed
design is that heterogeneous federated systems become in-
creasingly difficult to coordinate when local models differ
dramatically in size. In conventional federated learning, partic-
ipants transmit full parameter tensors during each round. For
large convolutional backbones, this communication dominates
system cost.
If participant Hi trains model with parameter count Pi, then
standard federated transmission cost per round is O(Pi). For
strong-capacity participants using ResNet-50, Pi ≈ 25.6 × 106
parameters, so at 32-bit precision the upload alone is:
25.6 × 106 × 4 bytes ≈ 102 MB (62)
yielding a round-trip of approximately 204 MB per partic-
ipant.
B. Metadata-Only Coordination Cost
The proposed architecture instead transmits per hospital per
round:
(modelHashi, Ci, Ei, modelT ypei)
= 4 × 32 bytes = 128 bytes (upload) (63)
Each hospital also reads back N on-chain weights (96 bytes
for N =3), giving a total round-trip of 224 bytes.
Table IX and Figure 7 summarise the per-hospital per-round
communication cost for all methods.

The design intentionally keeps all computationally heavy
objects local:
• local model parameters,
• local logits,
• local gradients,
• final ensemble probabilities.
Only four 32-byte scalars per hospital cross the coordination

to model size.

XVII. SECURITY AND TRUST MODEL
A. Threat Surface
Because the system separates on-chain policy from off-chain
learning, trust assumptions remain distributed across multiple
boundaries.
The relevant adversaries include:
• dishonest hospital participants,
• dishonest metric reporters,
• benchmark spoofers,
• stale model submitters.
B. What the Contract Defends Against
The Solidity layer provides deterministic protection against:
• duplicate submissions,
• unregistered participation,
• architecture mismatch,
• inconsistent round submission ordering.
These guarantees are strong because they are directly en-
coded in state transitions.
C. What the Contract Does Not Defend Against
Several critical properties remain externally trusted.
1) Benchmark Truthfulness: The contract verifies:
recover(signature, hB ) = addri (66)
but does not verify:
capacityi = f (preimage(hB )) (67)
Thus benchmark truthfulness is not cryptographically en-
forced.
2) Metric Inflation: Confidence and calibration are self-
reported:
(Ci, Ei) ← hospitali (68)
No independent validation currently exists.
A malicious participant may inflate confidence or under-
report calibration error.
3) Prediction Record Integrity: The contract stores:
predictionHash (69)
without proving derivation from actual submitted models.
Thus ensemble recording is audit metadata only.
D. Correct Security Positioning
The scientifically safe security statement is:
the current prototype binds declared benchmark and
submission metadata to participant identity while
leaving benchmark truthfulness and reliability cor-
rectness externally trusted
This wording avoids overstating trust guarantees.

XVIII. IMPLEMENTATION LIMITATIONS
A. Single-Host Heterogeneity
The main limitation of the current evaluation design is that
multiple hospitals execute on the same machine.
B. Python Mirror Versus Solidity Runtime
The main experiments mirror contract arithmetic in Python:
gpython ≡ gsolidity (70)
but do not execute Solidity inside every training round.
Thus reported results demonstrate policy semantics rather
than chain-executed throughput.
C. Protocol Demo Versus Model Evaluation
The blockchain demonstration path computes final ensemble
output using random tensor input.
Therefore that path validates protocol sequence rather than
predictive quality.
XIX. FUTURE RESEARCH DIRECTIONS
A. Trusted Benchmark Attestation
A stronger future design should bind benchmark semantics
directly to contract-verifiable preimages.
Possible directions include:
• trusted execution environments,
• remote attestation,
• benchmark committee verification.
B. Metric Verification
Reliability metrics should be externally checked.
Possible mechanisms:
• shared validation committees,
• encrypted validation pools,
• zero-knowledge metric proofs.
C. True Multi-Host Deployment
A scientifically stronger next stage would execute hospitals
across physically distinct hardware classes.
This would test whether benchmark classes remain stable
under genuine infrastructure diversity.
D. Byzantine-Robust Weighting
Future aggregation policy may penalize:
• confidence inflation,
• unstable participation,
• suspicious calibration behavior.
E. Contract-Level Prediction Binding
A stronger coordinator may require cryptographic linkage
between submitted model hashes and final prediction evidence.
XX. CONCLUSION
This work presents a heterogeneous federated learning co-
ordination architecture in which participant hardware capacity
determines local model family selection while blockchain ex-
ternalizes deterministic participation policy. Rather than forc-
ing all institutions into identical parameter spaces, the frame-
work assigns architecture according to measured throughput
and aggregates predictions through weighted probability-space
ensembling.
The central design decision is to separate policy from
numerical learning. Smart contracts store identity-bound meta-
data, round state, and deterministic weighting semantics, while
all heavy learning remains local and all inference remains
off chain. This yields an auditable coordination substrate that
preserves privacy and sharply reduces communication payload
relative to parameter-sharing federated systems.
Experimental evidence across PneumoniaMNIST and Der-
maMNIST shows that heterogeneous weighted ensembling
remains operationally viable under non-IID medical image
classification, with consistently better calibration than equal-
weight ensembling across all settings. Current prototype lim-
itations include weakly verified benchmark truthfulness and
self-reported reliability metrics.
The strongest scientific contribution is therefore not a new
optimization algorithm, but a systems architecture demonstrat-
ing that heterogeneous federated participation, deterministic
blockchain policy, and minimal metadata coordination can
coexist within a practical medical learning prototype.
